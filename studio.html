The Studio · Canvases

The canvases

On the ship's lower deck, Lucía turned an empty cargo hold into a painting studio. Every canvas here is painted by a rule from the voyage. You choose the colours and turn the dials; the arithmetic does the brushwork.

There are no right answers in the Studio. Play until something surprises you, then ask the crew's favourite question: why does it look like that?

How to keep a painting

Press Make a still under any canvas. The picture appears below the controls: right-click it (or press and hold on a phone) to save it. Share your best ones with your crew, along with the settings that made them.

Times-table circle

Put n points around a clock. Join each point k to point a·k mod n. Times 2 draws a heart-shaped cardioid; times 3 a three-petal curve. Try 21, 34, 51 and 99 with 200 points.

Chapter 3, Euclid's Forge

Euclid's quilt

Cut the biggest squares you can from an a × b cloth, then repeat on what is left. That is Euclid's algorithm, sewn: each colour is one step, the number of squares in a colour is a quotient, and the smallest square is the gcd. Try neighbouring Fibonacci numbers (89 × 55).

The Studio · Pixels · Chapter 8

The Pixel Room

Kevin swears he can destroy any picture with one simple rule, repeated: move the pixel at (x, y) to (x + y, x + 2y), wrapping around the edges mod N. After a few steps the picture is pure noise. Keep going, and something impossible happens.

Arnold's cat map

The mathematician Vladimir Arnold made this rule famous in the 1960s by scrambling a drawing of a cat. Every step is a shuffle of finitely many pixels, so some power of the shuffle must be the identity: the picture has to come back. The question is when.

Picture
Rule: (x, y) → (a·x + b·y, c·x + d·y) mod N
Rules to try

Why it comes back, and when

The rule is a matrix acting on pairs mod N. Doing it k times is the matrix to the power k, and the picture is back exactly when that power is the identity mod N. That k is the order of the matrix, the same idea as the order of a number in Chapter 8.

The cat map's matrix [[1, 1], [1, 2]] is the Fibonacci step [[0, 1], [1, 1]] done twice. So its period is half the Fibonacci tide length from the Poets' Corner: for N = 124 the Fibonacci numbers mod 124 repeat after 30 steps, and the cat comes back after 15.

Challenges

  1. Predict: for N = 101, after how many steps? (Check the table.) Why is a big N not always slower?
  2. Try the rule a = 2, b = 0, c = 0, d = 2 with N = 124. The picture loses pixels and never comes back. Why? (Look at gcd(ad − bc, N).)
  3. The quarter turn comes back after 4 steps for every N. Find a rule that always comes back after exactly 6. (Hint: a = 0, b = −1, and try small c and d.)
  4. Cartographer: the cat map stretches in one direction and squeezes in another. Why does that make it look so random in between?
The Studio · Gallery

The Gallery

On the last night of the voyage, the crew hangs its best work along the deck: paintings, poems, rhythms, cloth, ciphers. Every piece gets a card that says what it is, who made it, and the math that made it.

The one rule

A gallery card must explain the math in one or two sentences that a classmate from another crew could follow. The art is yours; the sentence is the proof that you understand it.

Make a gallery card

Your card appears here. Save it, and post it where your instructor collects the gallery (for example, a course discussion board).

Ideas by room

The gallery show

For instructors: the gallery fits the week 10 Expedition Festival, or a short show at the end of any week. Crews hang or project their cards; everyone walks the room with sticky notes and leaves one question or one "I noticed" on each card. Crews answer the questions in their logbook.

A gallery card can stand in for one Navigator problem, or count toward the expedition project's culture and art option.

What makes a strong card

  • The settings let someone else rebuild the piece.
  • The math sentence names the idea (gcd, lcm, a leftover, an order) and the numbers.
  • The picture description is specific enough to imagine it.

The Game Room · Board games

Board games

Some of the oldest games in the world are played around a circle, and circles mean leftovers. Below deck there is a carved mancala board and a crew that never tires of hot potato. Win them, then explain why you won.

Games from many places

Mancala games (oware in Ghana, bao in East Africa, and many more across Africa, the Middle East and South Asia) are among the oldest board games still played. The version here is Kalah, a simple modern variant that is easy to learn.

Mancala

Pick one of your pits (bottom row). Its seeds are sown one by one counterclockwise, into your store too, but never into the computer's store. Last seed in your store: go again. Last seed in an empty pit on your side: capture it and the seeds across from it. Most seeds in your store when one side runs out wins.

The leftover trick

Going around, there are 13 holes (12 pits and your store). From a pit that is d steps from your store, a pile of s seeds ends in your store exactly when s ≡ d (mod 13). Turn on Lucía's marks to see where each pile will end: “store!” means an extra turn.

Hot potato

n players stand in a circle. Counting starts at player 1; every k-th player is out, and counting goes on from the next player. Who is left at the end?

Find the pattern (Chapter 1)

With k = 2, list the survivor for n = 1, 2, …, 16. You should see runs that restart at 1 whenever n is a power of 2. Write n = 2m + L and guess the survivor in terms of L. Then prove it by strong induction.

History: this is the Josephus problem, named after a story told by the historian Flavius Josephus in the first century.

The Game Room · Juggling

The Juggling Yard

Every evening on deck, Mateo juggles whatever is lying around: limes, corks, the occasional copper flask. Jugglers write their tricks as strings of numbers called siteswaps, and whether a trick works at all is a question about leftovers.

New to this? Start with three examples. A siteswap like 441 means: on each beat, throw the ball in your hand so that it lands that many beats later. Hands take turns, right, left, right, left. The pattern repeats forever.

The two rules of the yard

Odd throws cross, even throws come back. A ball thrown on beat i lands on beat i + t, in the hand of that beat, so it changes hands exactly when t is odd. Parity, from Chapter 0.

No two balls land together. For a pattern of length p, the landing beats i + ti must all be different mod p. Congruences, from Chapter 6.

How to read a siteswap

Imagine a metronome ticking. On every tick (a beat) one hand throws, and the hands take turns: right, left, right, left. A siteswap is just a list of numbers, one per beat, that repeats forever. Each number says how many beats later the ball comes down.

  • Odd numbers land on a beat of the other hand, so the ball crosses.
  • Even numbers land back in the same hand.
  • Bigger numbers mean higher throws: the ball needs more time in the air.
  • 0 means the hand is empty on that beat; 2 means the hand just holds its ball for a moment.

Reading the pictures

In each ladder diagram below, the top row of dots is the right hand and the bottom row is the left hand. The small numbers next to the dots count the beats 0, 1, 2, … Each arc is one throw, from the beat it leaves to the beat it lands. Arcs that switch rows cross hands. Dashed arcs land past the edge of the picture.

Example 3: 441, two high, one across

Now the numbers change: two high throws that come back to the same hand (4, 4), then a quick pass straight across (1). The pattern 4, 4, 1 repeats.

The check: the throws on beats 0, 1, 2 land on beats 0 + 4 = 4, 1 + 4 = 5 and 2 + 1 = 3. Divide by the length 3: the leftovers are 1, 2 and 0, all different, so no two balls ever come down on the same beat. The average (4 + 4 + 1) ÷ 3 = 3 tells you it needs 3 balls.

Why the average is the number of balls

In 441 the average is (4 + 4 + 1) ÷ 3 = 3, and the trick uses 3 balls. In 5551 it is 16 ÷ 4 = 4 balls. This always works, and a pattern whose average is not a whole number can never be juggled.

Prove it (Navigator)

Once the pattern is running, every ball is always on exactly one arc of the ladder: the stretch from its last throw to its next one. Count the pairs (ball, beat) over one period of p beats in two ways. By beats: on each beat all n balls are on arcs, giving p × n. By throws: throw ti covers ti beats, giving t1 + … + tp. So the sum is p × n.

Average is not enough

Try 432: its average is 3, but the landing beats 0 + 4, 1 + 3, 2 + 2 are 4, 4, 4. Mod 3, all three balls land on the same beat. Crash.

A theorem from 1994 (Cartographer)

Count the siteswaps of length p that use fewer than b balls (counting rotations like 441, 414 and 144 separately). Use the finder for several p and b, and guess the formula before you open the hint.

Hint

The answer is a power. Buhler, Eisenbud, Graham and Wright proved it in "Juggling drops and descents" (1994). Mathematician Ron Graham was also a serious juggler.

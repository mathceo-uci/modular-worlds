Chapters 0, 1 and 2

The multiples machine

Each voice plays every a beats, starting on beat b: beats b, b + a, b + 2a, … That is a Book, as a rhythm. Start with even and odd beats, then ask: when do two voices play together?

Chapters 1 and 2

Phase clapping

Two people clap the same 12-beat rhythm. Every few rounds, clapper B jumps one beat ahead. The rhythms tangle into new patterns, and after 12 jumps they are together again. Composer Steve Reich built a famous piece, Clapping Music (1972), on this idea; here you make your own.

Your 12-beat rhythm (press beats to change it)

Ask why

After s jumps, clapper B plays the rhythm shifted by s mod 12. Why are they back together after 12 jumps, and never before (for most rhythms)? Find a rhythm that comes back together after only 4 jumps. What is special about it?

The Clockwork Isles

The note clock

A piano repeats every 12 keys, so musicians already count notes mod 12: C is 0, C♯ is 1, up to B, which is 11. Pick a step s and walk around the clock: 0, s, 2s, 3s, … mod 12, until you come home.

Listen, then ask why

Which steps visit all 12 notes before coming home? Which get stuck on 6, 4 or 3 notes? Find a rule with the gcd. Then compare steps 5 and 7: same notes, opposite order. Why? (Hint: 5 ≡ −7 mod 12.)

Chapter 3, Euclid's Forge

Euclid's drum circle

Spread k beats as evenly as possible over n pulses. The recipe for "as evenly as possible" is Euclid's algorithm in disguise, and the rhythms it makes, written E(k, n), are played all over the world.

Rhythms from around the world

Listen, then ask why

Try "3 against 4": the tracks start together, drift apart, and meet again. After how many pulses? Change the lengths and predict the meeting point before you press play. This is the lcm, and it is the heart of the Chinese Remainder Theorem in Chapter 8.

Clockwork Isles and Fermat's Lighthouse

Residue melodies

Lay m notes of a scale in a row, numbered 0 to m − 1. The melody plays note (a·k + b) mod m for k = 0, 1, 2, … and loops when the leftovers start to repeat.

Scale

Listen, then ask why

When does the melody use every note in the row before repeating? When does it get stuck on just a few? Compare a with m − a: the second melody is the first one turned upside down. Prove it.

Where the rhythms come from

The link between Euclid's algorithm and traditional rhythms was found by the computer scientist Godfried Toussaint, in "The Euclidean Algorithm Generates Traditional Musical Rhythms" (Bridges, 2005). The names of the rhythms above come from his paper. Rhythms are cycles, so a rhythm and its rotations count as the same "necklace": the presets start each one where musicians usually do.

Instructor guide · Week 3

Euclid's Forge

By the end of the week students can run Euclid's algorithm forwards and backwards, prove Lemma 3.1 and Bézout's identity, and explain exactly which weights two kinds of weights can balance.

Students read Chapter 3 across the week. Session A uses Explore and the balance, Session B the Theory, Session C the Arena and the Boss Problem. Problems and the code quest are due the next week.

Materials

  • Weight set (one per crew of 4): 10 red chips marked 5 (oxygen), 10 blue chips marked 7 (nitrogen), 1 black bead marked with the target. Magnetic chips on a whiteboard balance work well; paper squares work too.
  • Laptops: one per crew, with Chapter 3 open (the balance, the Euclid table, the game).
  • Whiteboards and markers: one per crew, for proof wrestling and the Boss Problem.
  • Prophecy Journals: crews log Prophecies 3.1–3.4 before Session B.
  • Projector and speakers for Session C's opener: the Sound Room drum circle. Press play once before class to check the sound.

Session plans (3 × 50 minutes)

A · Expedition

  1. 5 minEpisode. Read the opening scene aloud in parts (or play the episode video when it exists). Stop on Lucía's line.
  2. 15 minBalance 1 to 12. Crews use chips. Push for weights on both pans. Collect recipes on the board: 1 = 3·5 − 2·7.
  3. 12 minNew weights. 6 and 9, then 12 and 18. Ask: "Which beads can you never balance? Prove never." Crews seal Prophecies 3.1–3.4.
  4. 18 minEuclid by hand. Slow version (keep subtracting) on 252 and 198, then the remainder version. Ask why both give 18.

B · The Forge

  1. 12 minLemma 3.1. Crews try to prove it first; then write the proof together. Stress "same set of common divisors".
  2. 18 minEuclid backwards. Back-substitute 252 and 198 on the board, then crews do 391 and 299 using the page's table to check. State and prove Bézout.
  3. 15 minProof wrestling. Claims: "If d divides ax + by then d = gcd(a, b)"; "gcd(a + b, b) = gcd(a, b)"; "the Bézout x and y are unique".
  4. 5 minOpen Prophecies 3.1 and 3.2. Celebrate correct "will break" calls as loudly as correct "true" calls.

C · The Arena

  1. 5 minDrum circle opener. Project the Sound Room and choose Tresillo, E(3, 8). Play it, then the whole room claps the beats with it. Now ask each crew to predict where 5 beats in 8 pulses should go, as evenly as possible, and write it as x and dots. Play Cinquillo, E(5, 8) to check. Leave the question open: "How could spreading beats evenly be the same as Euclid's algorithm?" (See the note below.)
  2. 15 minEuclid's Game. Crews play each other on whiteboards, then against El Gran Pesado on the page. Collect winning and losing starting pairs on the board.
  3. 22 minBoss Problem. El Gran Pesado brings weights 391 and 299: balance their gcd and give the recipe. Every crew must finish to unlock Chapter 4 on the class map.
  4. 8 minCulture. Euclid, the Nine Chapters, Āryabhaṭa, Lamé. Discuss: who gets credit for an idea many cultures found?

Why the drum circle is Euclid's algorithm

Start with 3 beats and 5 rests: [x][x][x][·][·][·][·][·]. Glue one rest onto each beat: [x·][x·][x·] and [·][·] are left over. Glue the leftovers onto the groups again: [x··][x··] and [x·] are left. One group is left over, so stop: x··x··x·, the tresillo.

The group counts go 8 and 3, then 3 and 2, then 2 and 1: the same pairs Euclid's algorithm visits (8 = 2·3 + 2, 3 = 1·2 + 1). This is Bjorklund's algorithm, and Godfried Toussaint showed in 2005 that it produces many traditional rhythms: the tresillo, the cinquillo, the cumbia (E(3, 4)), the bossa nova (E(5, 16)) and a West African bell pattern (E(7, 12)). Good Cartographer question: why do the beats come out evenly spread?

Common mistakes and what to ask

What students doWhat to ask
Report the last remainder, 0, as the gcd."What does every number divide? So is 0 a useful answer?" The gcd is the last nonzero remainder.
Swap quotient and remainder, or let the remainder be larger than the divisor."Could you take one more copy of 198 out of 252?" Check 0 ≤ r < b on every line.
Lose a sign when running Euclid backwards."Check your recipe: compute 252x + 198y. Do you get 18?" Always verify at the end.
Think the Bézout coefficients are unique."Add 7 oxygen weights and take away 5 nitrogen weights. Still balanced?" (x + 7k, y − 5k works for every k.)
Think "d divides ax + by" means d is the gcd."Take x = y = 0. What divides 0?"
Prove "never" with examples: "I tried 30 recipes for 1 with 6 and 9.""What do all totals 6x + 9y have in common?" (They are multiples of 3.)
In Euclid's Game, always take the biggest multiple."Is there a position where taking one fewer copy wins instead?"

Answer keys

Boss Problem

391 = 1·299 + 92 299 = 3·92 + 23 92 = 4·23 + 0 gcd = 23 23 = 299 − 3·92 = 299 − 3·(391 − 299) = 4·299 − 3·391

Balance: four 299-weights on one pan, three 391-weights and the bead of 23 on the other.

Prophecies

  • 3.1 Fulfilled: gcd(5, 7) = 1 (Corollary 3.3).
  • 3.2 Fulfilled: every 6x + 9y is a multiple of 3, and 3 = −1·6 + 1·9.
  • 3.3 Fulfilled: the maximum for numbers up to 1000 is 14 steps, at 987 and 610 (consecutive Fibonacci numbers).
  • 3.4 Fulfilled: a + b = 1·b + a, so gcd(a + b, b) = gcd(b, a) = 1 by Lemma 3.1.

Deckhand

  1. gcd(1071, 462) = 21 = −3·1071 + 7·462 (steps: 1071 = 2·462 + 147, 462 = 3·147 + 21, 147 = 7·21).
  2. x = 12, y = −8, or the shorter x = −2, y = 2.
  3. Write a = gk and b = gl with g = gcd(a, b). Then a − b = g(k − l).
  4. No: gcd(12, 18) = 6 does not divide 50.

Navigator

  1. If gcd = 1, Bézout gives ax + by = 1. Conversely, if ax + by = 1, every common divisor divides 1, so the gcd is 1.
  2. Fn+1 = 1·Fn + Fn−1, so gcd(Fn+1, Fn) = gcd(Fn, Fn−1) by Lemma 3.1. By induction this equals gcd(F2, F1) = gcd(1, 1) = 1.
  3. By Bézout, gcd(a, b) = ax + by, and d divides both terms.
  4. Broken: with x = y = 0, every d divides ax + by = 0. A correct version: the positive values of ax + by are exactly the multiples of gcd(a, b).

Cartographer

  1. Slowest pairs. Consecutive Fibonacci numbers (Lamé, 1844). Proof idea: if Euclid takes n steps on a > b, then b ≥ Fn+1 and a ≥ Fn+2, by induction on n.
  2. Euclid's Game. With a ≥ b, the player to move wins exactly when a = b or a/b > φ = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618. If a ≥ 2b the mover can choose between leaving (a mod b + b, b) and (b, a mod b), and one of them is a losing position for the opponent. If b < a < 2b there is one forced move to (b, a − b), and a/b < φ exactly when b/(a − b) > φ.
  3. Three weights. gcd(6, 10, 15) = 1 and 1 = 6 + 10 − 15, so every bead balances, even though no two of the weights are coprime. In general the balanceable beads are the multiples of gcd(a, b, c).

If you run short

  • Session A behind: do "New weights" as a 3-minute whole-class demo on the projected balance, then seal the prophecies.
  • Session B behind: do one proof-wrestling claim instead of three; keep the prophecy opening.
  • Session C behind: keep the 5-minute opener short (tresillo only, no prediction). Euclid's Game becomes homework (it is on the page). Never cut the Boss Problem: it unlocks the next land for the whole class.

If a crew finishes early

  • Cartographer problem 2 (Euclid's Game rule).
  • Code quest cell 3: change recipe() to return the recipe with the fewest weights.
  • Sound Room: build a 3-track rhythm whose tracks line up only every 24 pulses, then explain why with the lcm.
  • Preview Chapter 4: with only nonnegative weights on one pan, which beads of 5s and 7s can be built?

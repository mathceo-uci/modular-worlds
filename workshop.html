Calendar Isles · lcm and the Chinese Remainder Theorem

Bead bracelets

A bracelet has n spots. You thread a colour pattern of length p again and again: bead number k gets colour k mod p and lands on spot k mod n. If p does not divide n, the first lap has a seam. Keep going around: when does the pattern close perfectly?

Pattern colours

Ask why

With 12 spots and a 5-bead pattern, every spot ends up wearing every colour exactly once. With 12 spots and a 4-bead pattern, each spot only ever sees one colour. Predict, for any n and p, how many colours each spot sees. This is a first glimpse of the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

Clockwork Isles · gcd and units

The loom

In a satin weave, each weft row lifts one warp thread out of every n, and each new row moves the lift s threads over: row j lifts thread s·j mod n. Satin feels smooth because the lifts are scattered, never touching. Weavers' rule: s must share no factor with n, and must not be 1 or n − 1. Try to see why.

Ask why

There is no true satin with n = 4 or n = 6. Prove it. Then explain what goes wrong when gcd(s, n) > 1: look for warp threads that are never lifted, and imagine pulling on them.

Cords hang from a main cord. Lowest position: units. Above it: tens, hundreds, thousands. A cluster of single knots shows a digit; in the units place a long knot with that many turns is used instead, and a figure-eight knot means 1. An empty position means 0.

Chapters 0 and 1 · place value is the division algorithm

The khipu

In the Inka Empire, record keepers called khipukamayuq stored numbers in knotted cords. Each digit is a leftover: the units digit of n is n mod 10, the tens digit is (n div 10) mod 10, and so on. Tie your own khipu, then read one.

The Workshop · Gears · Chapter 3

The Gear Shop

In the smiths' quarter of Euclid's Forge, an old clockmaker sells toothed rings and wheels. Put a pen through a hole in a small wheel, roll it around inside a big ring, and a flower appears. How many petals? When does the pen come home? The answers are a gcd and an lcm.

The gear rule

A wheel with r teeth rolling in a ring with R teeth draws a figure with R ÷ gcd(R, r) petals, and closes after r ÷ gcd(R, r) trips around the ring.

Why it closes: count teeth

Each time the wheel rolls one tooth, one tooth of the ring has been used. The pen is back where it started exactly when the wheel has turned a whole number of times and the contact point is back at the start of the ring. So the number of teeth rolled must be a multiple of r and of R: the first time is lcm(R, r) teeth.

That many teeth is lcm ÷ R trips around the ring, and lcm ÷ r turns of the wheel. Each turn of the wheel draws one petal. Since lcm(R, r) = Rr ÷ gcd(R, r), you get the gear rule.

Challenges

  1. Find a ring and wheel that make exactly 5 petals. Then find three more pairs that also make 5.
  2. Which wheel gives the most petals for a ring of 96? Which gives the fewest (other than a full-size wheel)?
  3. With R = 105 and r = 64, how many trips before the pen comes home? Predict, then press Draw slowly.
  4. Navigator: prove that the petals for (R, r) and for (R, R − r) are the same number.

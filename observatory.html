The Observatory

On the Calendar Isles, an observatory keeps time the way the Maya did: with wheels inside wheels. A 13-day count turns with 20 day names to make the 260-day Tzolk'in. Beside it turns the 365-day Haab'. Together they make the Calendar Round, which repeats every 52 years.

Three cycles, one question

How long until all the wheels line up again? That is an lcm. Which combinations can ever happen at all? That is the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and the answer depends on a gcd.

Inner ring: the 13 numbers. Middle ring: the 20 day names. Outer ring: the 365 days of the Haab' (18 months of 20 days and 5 days of Wayeb'). The mark at the top is today's position.

Your birthday in the Maya calendar

How the lookup works

Every date gets a day number, counted from a fixed starting day long ago. Then each cycle is just a leftover: the weekday is the day number mod 7, the Tzolk'in uses it mod 13 and mod 20, and the Haab' uses it mod 365.

The link between the Maya count and our calendar is the GMT correlation (Maya day zero = day number 584,283), the one most scholars use. The 260-day count is still kept today by K'iche' daykeepers in highland Guatemala.

Do they ever meet?

Pick a Tzolk'in date and a Haab' date. Will they ever fall on the same day?

The CRT at work

The Tzolk'in repeats every 260 days and the Haab' every 365. A pair can only happen if the two positions agree mod gcd(260, 365) = 5. So only one pair in five ever occurs: 18,980 pairs out of 94,900.

Cartographer: the Haab' day names that can go with a Tzolk'in day name are called "year bearers" when they start a year. Show that only 4 of the 20 day names can ever start a Haab' year.

The week is a wheel too

Quick rule

Days of the week repeat every 7, so only n mod 7 matters. 1,000 days from a Wednesday: 1,000 = 7 × 142 + 6, so six days after Wednesday.

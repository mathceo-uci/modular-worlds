The Poets' Corner

In the ship's library, Sofía keeps a notebook of poems with rules. Some forms are older than algebra, and their rules are modular arithmetic in disguise: words that rotate on a schedule, lines that grow and shrink with the tides of a sequence.

You may write in English, in Spanish or in both. Your lines stay in this browser only; nobody else sees them unless you copy and share them.

The rule of the corner

A form is a constraint, and constraints are where ideas come from. The math tells you where each word must land. What the poem says is all yours.

Chapter 0 · even and odd

A poem for two voices

Two readers share one poem: the first reads the odd lines, the second the even ones. Line n goes to the voice given by its leftover, n mod 2. With three voices, use n mod 3, like Doña Trina's trios.

Voices

Perform it

Split your crew into voices and read it aloud. Then predict without counting: in a 25-line poem, who reads the last line? Who reads more lines, and how many more? Try 3 voices with 25 lines.

Chapters 0 and 1 · divisors and the division algorithm

Stanza crates

Pack a poem's lines into stanzas of equal size, like the Crate Market packs lanterns. If the size divides the number of lines, every stanza is full. If not, the lines left over become a short coda at the end: n = q × d + r.

Ask why

A 12-line poem can be packed 6 ways with no coda. A 13-line poem only 2 ways. Which line counts up to 30 have the most ways? Which have the fewest? Name them.

Clockwork Isles · permutations

The sestina machine

A sestina has six stanzas of six lines. It never rhymes; instead, six end-words close the lines, and they move from stanza to stanza by one fixed shuffle, the spiral: take the last word, then the first, then the second-to-last, then the second, and so on.

Troubadour poets in 12th-century Provence (Arnaut Daniel is credited with the first) used it. In the 20th century the French writer and mathematician Raymond Queneau asked: for which numbers of words n does the spiral shuffle bring every word through every position before coming home?

Your end-words

Listen to the numbers

With 6 words, every word visits every position once, and the seventh stanza would be the first again. Try 4 words: the shuffle splits into smaller cycles and comes home early. Find all n up to 12 that work like 6 (the Queneau numbers). Bonus: there is a beautiful test using mod 2n + 1. Can you find it?

Write a sestina

Each line already knows its end-word. Write toward it. The three-line envoi at the end uses all six words, two per line. Skip lines, come back, change your words above: your lines are kept.

Fibonacci, mod m

Fibonacci tides

A Fib is a short poem whose lines have 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 syllables: each count is the sum of the two before. Now take the counts mod m. The numbers rise and fall like a tide, and, surprisingly, the tide always comes back to 1, 1. The length of one full tide is the Pisano period.

Form
Syllable counter

The counter is a rough guide (English and Spanish words are both full of exceptions). If your ear disagrees with it, trust your ear. A line with 0 syllables is a breath: leave it empty.

Why does the tide return?

There are only m² possible pairs of neighbours mod m, so some pair must repeat. Each pair decides the next number, and it also decides the one before (subtract!). Use that to prove the tide comes back to 1, 1, and not just to some later pair.

Patterns that loop

The pantoum

A pantoum comes from Malay poetry (the pantun). Lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza brings back the first stanza's lines 3 and 1, so the poem ends where it began. You only write the new lines.

Count it

A pantoum with s stanzas has 4s lines but only 2s different ones, and every line appears exactly twice. Prove it by counting the new lines in each stanza.

Counting and place value

A hundred thousand billion poems

In 1961 Raymond Queneau published ten sonnets of 14 lines, cut into strips so that any line can be swapped for the same line of another sonnet. That makes 1014 poems, a hundred thousand billion. Here is a small version: write several versions of each line.

Poem numbers are place value

With 4 versions of each of 4 lines, write the poem number in base 4: its digits say which version of each line to use. Poem 0 is all first versions; poem 255 is all last versions. Why does base 4 give exactly one poem for each number from 0 to 255?

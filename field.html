How the station works

Each chapter has field cards. A card has:

  • a field where the idea is used (computer science, engineering, biology, astronomy, chemistry, games);
  • a small instrument to try;
  • Back to the math: the theorem from the chapter that makes it work;
  • a field question for your crew.

The Proof Lab asks why something is true. The Studio asks what it looks and sounds like. The Field Station asks where it is used.

Chapter 0 · The Entrance Exam: even and odd

Computer science

The parity bit

Computers send letters as strings of 0s and 1s. A simple safety check adds one extra parity bit so that the total number of 1s is even. If one bit flips on the way, the count turns odd, and the receiver knows something went wrong.

Press bits to change the message. The last bit (gold border) is the parity bit, set automatically.

Back to the math: adding a 1 changes even to odd and odd to even (the plaza rules, Theorem 0.1).

Field question: what happens if two bits flip? Can parity catch it? What would you add to catch it?

Networks

Cables between computers

Picture a network: computers joined by cables. Count, for each computer, how many cables plug into it. Some counts are odd. However the network is built, the number of computers with an odd count is always even.

Each cable has two ends, so the counts add up to twice the number of cables, an even number. A sum of numbers is even only when it has an even number of odd terms.

Back to the math: the handshake rule (Theorem 0.1′), the same rule that tells chemists which molecules can close up.

Field question: someone claims to have built a network of 7 routers, each connected to exactly 3 others. Why are they wrong?

Games

The impossible 15 puzzle

In the sliding 15 puzzle, swap the 14 and 15 tiles and try to slide everything back. You can't, ever. In 1879 William Johnson and William Story proved that exactly half of all arrangements can be solved and half cannot.

Count the pairs of tiles in the wrong order, and add the blank's row. A sideways slide changes neither. An up-or-down slide moves the blank one row and changes the wrong-order count by 1 or 3. So the total's parity never changes, but swapping 14 and 15 changes it by 1.

Back to the math: an invariant, the same argument as the Broken Floor.

Field question: a Rubik's cube with one corner twisted can't be solved either. Look up why. Is it a parity argument too?

Chapter 1 · Mojadito: division and induction

Computer science

Seconds to days

Your phone stores time as one huge count of seconds. To show you "3 days, 4 hours", it runs the division algorithm again and again: divide by 60, keep the remainder, divide the quotient by 60, then by 24.

Back to the math: each step is n = q·d + r with 0 ≤ r < d (Theorem 1.1). The remainders are the clock's digits.

Field question: one billion seconds is about how many years? Guess first, then convert.

Astronomy

The leap-year rule

A year (equinox to equinox) lasts about 365.2422 days. The Gregorian calendar (1582) adds a leap day in years divisible by 4, except years divisible by 100, except years divisible by 400. That averages 365.2425 days.

Back to the math: "divisible by" means remainder 0 in the division algorithm (Theorem 1.1).

Field question: the rule drifts by about 0.0003 days a year. Roughly when will the calendar be one full day off?

Computer science

Recursion is induction

To move a tower of n disks (the Tower of Hanoi, Édouard Lucas, 1883): move the top n − 1 out of the way, move the big disk, move the n − 1 back on top. Programmers call this recursion: a program that calls itself on a smaller case.

Moves needed: 1, 3, 7, 15, … Induction proves the pattern 2n − 1: if n − 1 disks take 2n−1 − 1 moves, then n disks take 2(2n−1 − 1) + 1 = 2n − 1.

Back to the math: a recursive program is correct for the same reason an induction proof works: the base case, then the step.

Field question: legend says monks move 64 disks, one per second. How long would that take?

Chapter 2 · Ammonia Bay: gcd and lcm

Biology

When two cicada broods meet

Some periodical cicadas emerge every 13 years, others every 17. Two neighbouring broods emerge together only every lcm(13, 17) = 221 years. In 2024, Brood XIII (17-year) and Brood XIX (13-year) came out together in Illinois, for the first time since 1803.

1803 + 221 = 2024. With 12-year and 18-year cycles, two broods would meet every lcm(12, 18) = 36 years instead.

Back to the math: two Books meet every lcm of their steps (Theorem 2.1).

Field question: when will these two broods next emerge together?

Engineering

The hunting tooth

When two gears turn, the same pairs of teeth keep meeting. If one tooth is damaged, it wears the same partners again and again. Engineers pick tooth counts with gcd 1 so every tooth meets every other tooth, spreading the wear (the extra tooth is called a hunting tooth).

Back to the math: the gears return to their start after lcm(a, b) tooth-steps, and each tooth of A meets b / gcd(a, b) teeth of B (Chapter 2). See them turn in the Gear Shop.

Field question: a bike has 48 teeth in front and 16 at the back. Is that a good choice for wear? What changes with 17?

Engineering

Lights that blink together

A lighthouse flashes every 12 seconds and a buoy every 20. When do they flash together? Traffic engineers ask the same question to sync signal cycles along a street.

Back to the math: lcm(a, b) = ab / gcd(a, b) (Chapter 2).

Field question: if the buoy starts 4 seconds after the lighthouse, do they ever flash together? (Chapter 2 has a test for "never".)

Chapter 3 · Euclid's Forge: Euclid and Bézout

Computer science

Screen shapes

A screen of 1920 × 1080 pixels is called "16:9". To get there, divide both by their gcd, 120. Video and photo apps reduce ratios this way every time they crop.

Back to the math: Euclid's algorithm finds the gcd in a few divisions, even for huge numbers.

Field question: a phone screen is 2532 × 1170. What is its ratio? Why isn't it a "nice" one?

Astronomy

Leap years from Euclid

The year's extra 0.2422 days must be packed into whole leap days. Running Euclid's algorithm on 2422 and 10000 gives the best simple choices: 1 leap day in 4 years, 7 in 29, 8 in 33, 31 in 128. Calendars associated with Omar Khayyam use 8 leap years every 33.

Back to the math: the quotients of Euclid's algorithm build these fractions, called continued fraction convergents.

Field question: a month (new moon to new moon) is about 29.5306 days. Find a good fraction for 0.5306 and invent a lunar calendar.

Computer science

The first speed test of an algorithm

In 1844, Gabriel Lamé proved that Euclid's algorithm needs at most about five steps per decimal digit of the smaller number. It is one of the first proofs about how fast an algorithm is, a question at the heart of computer science today.

The slowest inputs are neighbouring Fibonacci numbers: 89 and 55 take 9 divisions, because almost every quotient is 1.

Back to the math: each step of Euclid's algorithm shrinks the pair at least as fast as Fibonacci numbers grow.

Field question: your phone runs Euclid on numbers with 600 digits during a secure connection. About how many steps can that take, at most?

Chapter 4 · Diophantine Delta: whole-number equations

Puzzles and engineering

Measuring with two jugs

You have a 3-litre jug and a 5-litre jug, no markings, and a tap. Can you measure exactly 4 litres? Filling and pouring adds and subtracts the jug sizes, so you can measure c exactly when 3x + 5y = c has a solution.

Back to the math: possible exactly when gcd(a, b) divides c and c fits in the bigger jug (Theorem 4.2 and Bézout).

Field question: with jugs of 4 and 6 litres, which amounts can you never measure? Why?

Chemistry

Balancing a reaction

Propane burns in a gas grill: x C3H8 + y O2 → z CO2 + w H2O. Atoms are never created or destroyed, so each element balances, and you can't use part of a molecule.

Carbon: 3x = z. Hydrogen: 8x = 2w. Oxygen: 2y = 2z + w. Smallest whole solution: C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O.

Back to the math: a system of linear Diophantine equations (Chapter 4); every other solution is a multiple of the smallest.

Field question: balance the reaction that makes rust: Fe + O2 → Fe2O3.

Sports

Impossible scores

Suppose a football team scores only field goals (3 points) and touchdowns with the extra point (7 points). Some totals can't happen: 1, 2, 4, 5, 8 and 11. Every total above 11 can.

3 · 7 − 3 − 7 = 11, and (3 − 1)(7 − 1)/2 = 6 impossible totals. Real football also has 2-point safeties and conversions, and then every total except 1 is possible.

Back to the math: Sylvester's toll theorem (Corollary 4.4).

Field question: in rugby union, a try with conversion is 7, a try alone is 5 and a penalty is 3. Which totals are impossible?

Chapter 5 · Prime Peaks: primes

Computer science

Why hash tables like primes

Programs store data in a table of m slots, putting key k in slot k mod m. Real keys often come in steps (prices in tens, IDs in hundreds). With a bad table size, they pile into a few slots and searches get slow.

Back to the math: keys in steps of s use only m / gcd(s, m) slots. A prime size m has gcd 1 with every step it doesn't divide (Chapter 5).

Field question: try size 10 and then 11 with steps of 10. Which table size would you choose for prices in cents?

Cryptography

Easy to multiply, hard to undo

Multiplying two primes takes a moment. Undoing it, finding the primes from their product, can take ages. The security of the internet (Chapter 9) rests on this gap.

Back to the math: unique factorization (Theorem 5.5) says the two primes are the only answer, and the sieve bound (Proposition 5.3) says how far trial division must search.

Field question: real keys use primes with about 300 digits. If trial division checked a trillion numbers per second, roughly how long would it take?

Biology

Prime-year cicadas

Periodical cicadas emerge every 13 or 17 years, both prime. One hypothesis: a predator with a 2-, 3-, 4- or 6-year cycle would rarely line up with a prime cycle, but often with a 12-year one. Biologists still debate it.

A 12-year cicada meets a 4-year predator every lcm(12, 4) = 12 years, every single emergence. A 13-year cicada meets it every lcm(13, 4) = 52 years.

Back to the math: a prime p shares no factor with smaller cycles, so lcm(p, k) = pk for 1 < k < p (primes and lcm).

Field question: which cycle length from 10 to 20 years would meet predators of cycles 2 to 6 least often? Make a table.

Chapter 6 · Clockwork Isles: congruences and check digits

Publishing

From ISBN-10 to the barcode on a book

Since 1 January 2007, books carry 13-digit ISBNs, which double as barcodes. An old 10-digit ISBN becomes a new one by putting 978 in front, dropping the old check digit, and computing a new one on the 10-clock with weights 1, 3, 1, 3, …

Back to the math: the old check works on the 11-clock and catches every swap (Theorem 6.8); the new one works on the 10-clock and misses some (Theorem 6.9). Publishers traded strength for compatibility with grocery scanners.

Field question: why can't the old check digit simply be kept? Find an ISBN-10 whose two check digits are different.

Finance

Is this card number possible?

Before a payment is even sent, a website checks the card number with Luhn's rule (a patent from 1960). A typo is caught on your screen, not at the bank. The check says nothing about whether the card exists or has money.

Back to the math: doubling-and-adding sends the ten digits to ten different digits, so every typo moves the sum off 0 (Luhn's doubling trick).

Field question: 4111 1111 1111 1111 is a well-known test number that passes. Change one digit: can you find a change that still passes? Why not?

Chemistry

Chemical ID numbers

Every substance in the CAS Registry has a number like 7732-18-5 (water). Labs, suppliers and safety sheets use it, because names are ambiguous. The last digit is a check digit: weights 1, 2, 3, … from the right, on the 10-clock.

Back to the math: a typo of size e at weight w goes unnoticed exactly when 10 | we, so only weights that are units mod 10 are fully safe (Theorem 6.5).

Field question: the swap 18 → 81 above is caught. Which swaps of neighbours can a CAS check miss?

Computer science

The computer clock that runs out in 2038

Many systems store time as a signed 32-bit count of seconds since 1 January 1970. That count lives on a clock of 232 marks, from −231 to 231 − 1. One second after the last mark, it wraps around to the most negative one, which reads as December 1901.

Back to the math: 32-bit arithmetic is arithmetic mod 232; adding 1 to the largest mark lands on the smallest (Theorem 6.1). Systems are moving to 64-bit counts.

Field question: a 64-bit count of seconds wraps after 263 seconds. Roughly how many years is that?
